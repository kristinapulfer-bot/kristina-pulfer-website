Methods

Planting for the next hundred years.

I do not impose a preconceived style on a garden. Every project begins with looking, learning, and interpreting a site — its sun, its water, its existing plant life — before a single plant is chosen. Whether the project is a private garden or a civic planting, the aim is the same: a landscape wholly right for its place, one that only becomes more itself with time, asks less of its owners each year, and gives back to the ecology around it.

“It is the journey to find out what real beauty is, and to discover beauty in things that might not on first sight seem beautiful.”
Piet Oudolf

Philosophy

Rather than forcing a preconceived geometry onto a plot, my methodology seeks a dialogue with the existing environment, topography, and wild native flora — genius loci, the spirit of place, made legible in the plan.

My core design framework breaks down into a few practical, step-by-step methods.

1. The Ecological Layering System

I mimic natural ecosystems like woodlands and hedgerows by categorizing plants into interdependent vertical and chronological tiers. This structural layering delivers year-round succession, so that as one plant fades, another takes its place.

  • High Canopy: Trees that dictate the light and shadow structure of the garden (e.g., poplar or amelanchier).
  • Mid-Layer: Sub-trees and larger, human-scale deciduous or evergreen shrubs (e.g., ash, hawthorn).
  • Perennial & Ground Layer: Low-growing flora, ferns, and spring bulbs that blanket the ground before shading occurs (e.g., wood asters, hellebores, wild garlic).
  • Climbers: Interwoven plants like Clematis viticella that use the upper framework, anchoring their roots in the cool shade.

2. Deep Site Reading & Tracking

Before drawing a plan, I advocate for slow observation — walking a site across a full season, sometimes longer, before committing to major design moves.

  • Tracking the Sun: Charting the seasonal arc of light to position seating zones — placing evening chairs where they catch backlit, low sunset rays.
  • Low-Tech Analysis: Physically pushing a garden fork into the ground to judge soil composition, dampness, and microclimates.
  • Hydrological Framing: Mapping natural spring lines or wet hollows to build distinct plant communities — gravel gardens on dry areas, moisture-lovers in wet ditches.

3. Asymmetric Curation over Symmetry

I build dynamic, fluid spaces using naturalistic layout strategies instead of rigid, formal patterns.

  • The Gauzy Quality: Using plants with open habits and fine textures — like grasses or verbena — to create a wild, transparent veil through which visitors view the landscape.
  • Mass Plantings: Reducing the overall variety of species — ten tightly curated varieties instead of thirty — and repeating them in large, asymmetric waves.
  • Anchor Points: Placing strategic, heavy groups of static plants to serve as permanent structural weights amid shifting meadows.

4. Conceal & Reveal Journeys

A fundamental part of my planning is making the path between zones emotionally meaningful.

  • The Element of Surprise: Designing pathways that snake through gaps in hedges or hidden doorways to create a sense of discovery upon entering a new section.
  • Prospect and Refuge: Balancing elevated vantage points, where you can look over a landscape, with enclosed, heavily planted clearings that evoke security.

The Standard

A resilient garden isn't a romantic ideal. It's a practical commitment: to plants that perform honestly with the water and light they're actually given, to communities that tolerate a missed watering rather than punishing it, and to gardens that age into their site rather than against it. Done well, it becomes a world of its own, wholly right for its place — offering the chance for escape, delight, reflection, and habitat in return.

I apply this standard to every project, at every scale — from a single native bed to a civic planting. If a plant can't earn its place over a full season, I look for one that can.
